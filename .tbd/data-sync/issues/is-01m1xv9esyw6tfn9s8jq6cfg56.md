The anti-abandonment feature, straight out of reader research (docs/research-reader-experience.md).

Three documented failure modes, one answer:

1. Book VI 'The Russian Monk' is where readers most often stall — even critics concede it is slow and didactic next to Book V. Yet readers who describe the novel as life-changing name Book VI specifically. The section people skip is the section that pays. Nobody tells them.

2. Readers cannot tell a load-bearing digression from ornament, so they attend to everything and exhaust themselves. The anxiety is uncertainty, not length.

3. The narrator knows too much, forgets things, and becomes a character. First-time readers read this as sloppiness because nobody told them it is deliberate.

Build: a per-chapter orientation strip answering 'where am I in the argument, what is this answering, and why is it here'. For Book VI specifically: this is the reply to Ivan's Rebellion and the Grand Inquisitor; skipping it leaves you with the accusation and no defence.

Must not spoil. It says why a section exists, never what happens in it.
